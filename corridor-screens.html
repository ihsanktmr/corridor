01

Welcome & auth real

Get someone to a funded, non-custodial wallet without them ever learning they now have one.

  • Email or passkey. Nothing else. No seed phrase, no extension, no "connect wallet" — Privy provisions the embedded wallet on first login and shards the key across device, Privy, and recovery.
  • The word "wallet" never appears above the fold. It's in the footnote, as reassurance, not as a concept the user has to learn before signing up.
  • The positioning line is the hero. A remittance sender's real fear is that value shrinks in transit; the line answers it in one sentence with no crypto vocabulary.
  • The live rate is on the first screen. Rate is the single number this audience shops on — making them sign up to see it is a conversion tax.
  • Demo disclosure sits in the footer of the very first screen, permanently.

architecture

Privy useLoginWithEmail → embedded wallet on first auth. The wallet address is derived before the user reaches Home, so the balance query on 02 never races the wallet creation. Passkey is the fallback for returning users, not the primary — email survives device loss.

02

Home real rate feed

One balance, one corridor, one action. Everything else is history.

  • A single primary CTA. Not "send / receive / swap / buy" — this app does one thing, and the home screen should be embarrassed to pretend otherwise.
  • "≈ $1,284.50 — always 1:1" under the balance. The whole reason a remittance user tolerates a stablecoin is that it doesn't move. Say it where the number is.
  • The corridor strip carries the live rate plus its direction, so the user builds a mental model of the market before they're asked to commit to a number on 03.
  • Address is visible but demoted — mist, mono, copyable. Present for the crypto-literate, invisible to everyone else.
  • Activity uses status pills, not icons. in transit is a first-class state here because the ACH leg means transfers genuinely live for days.
  • The demo strip is persistent chrome, not a dismissible toast.

architecture

Balance reads from chain via Privy's provider; activity is a merge of on-chain transfers and off-chain Bridge transfer records, keyed on a client-generated transferId. Those two sources settle at different speeds, so the list renders from the off-chain record and enriches with the hash when it lands — never the reverse, or rows would appear and vanish.

03

Amount hybrid

The quote is the product. Everything else is logistics.

  • Mid-market rate, no markup — stated on the screen. Legacy remitters bury a 3–5% spread in a "free transfer". Naming our margin out loud is the entire competitive posture, so it goes next to the number, not in a help article.
  • The rate lock has a visible countdown. A quote that silently expires is the most common way a transfer flow loses trust. If it's going to expire, the user watches it happen.
  • Fees expand, they don't hide. Three line items, each named after the rail it pays for — on-ramp, network, payout. A CTO reading this sees the cost structure of the corridor.
  • Custom keypad, not the OS keyboard. The receive amount recomputes on every keystroke; a system keyboard would resize the viewport and make the two amounts jump. It also lets us reject a second decimal point rather than validate after the fact.
  • The mark is the divider between the two currencies — the identity's own applied pattern, doing its job.
  • "Elif receives", not "recipient gets". The recipient is already known from the last transfer.

architecture

Quotes are server-issued and signed, never computed on the client. The client displays a quoteId with an expiresAt; 08 submits the quoteId, not the amounts. This is what stops a stale or tampered rate from reaching settlement — the client's numbers are a rendering of the quote, never the source of it.

04

Add USD — on-ramp simulated

Make the speed-versus-cost trade legible before the user commits to it.

  • A date, not a duration. "Arrives Wed, Aug 5" instead of "1–3 business days" — the vague version is where remittance apps lose people, because the sender usually has a real deadline on the other end.
  • ACH is the default and it's free. Instant costs 1.5%. We show both honestly and let the user decide whether their urgency is worth $7.56.
  • The mini route preview shows which leg they're buying — the choice only affects the first hop; the chain and payout legs are identical either way.
  • The simulation notice is in the flow, at the moment of the lie. It names why (KYB gating) and what's still faithful (timing, fee structure).

architecture — the important one

ACH is the settlement-risk leg, and it's the reason this corridor is hard. An ACH debit can be returned days after it appears to have cleared — R01 insufficient funds up to 2 days, R08 stop payment, R10 unauthorised up to 60 days. The USDC has been sent and the TRY paid out long before that window closes. Every operator has to pick: hold the payout until ACH settles (slow, safe) or front the funds and underwrite the return risk (fast, needs capital and fraud scoring). Corridor holds. That's why 09 shows "USD received" as a discrete step with its own timestamp rather than collapsing it into a spinner — the wait is real, so the UI makes it legible instead of hiding it.

05

KYC gate hybrid

Explain before demanding. A verification wall with no stated reason reads as a scam to exactly the users who most need this product.

  • Placed after the quote, before funding. The user has already seen what they'd get, so they're verifying for something concrete. Gating at signup instead would kill the funnel before the value is visible.
  • Names the actual reason — US money transmission — rather than "for security reasons". Diaspora senders have usually been asked for documents by someone who shouldn't have asked; specificity is what separates us from that.
  • "Once. Not every transfer." The single most valuable sentence on the screen.
  • Says what we don't keep, unprompted. Data handling stated before the camera opens, not in a policy nobody opens.
  • Time estimate is concrete, and "Not now" is a real exit — the quote survives, the transfer parks.

architecture

KYC status is a server-owned state machine — none → pending → review → approved | rejected | resubmit — never inferred client-side. The client polls or subscribes; it never decides. Approval is also tiered: basic KYC clears the $2,999 per-transfer limit shown on 03, higher limits require proof of address and source of funds. Turkey adds a second constraint the flow has to respect — the payout name must match the verified sender or a declared recipient, which is why 07 warns about name matching rather than discovering it at payout time.

06

ID capture hybrid

One instruction at a time, with the camera doing the teaching.

  • The guide frame is the instruction. Two short lines of copy, not a paragraph. Anything longer gets skipped by a user holding a phone in one hand and a licence in the other.
  • Glare and blur are checked on-device before upload. Round-tripping a bad frame to a vendor and failing 60 seconds later is the single worst moment in any KYC flow — most abandonment happens on the second rejection.
  • The progress rail is three segments, not a percentage. The user needs to know how much is left, not how much is done.
  • "front captured" stays visible so the user knows what's banked. Nothing they've already done should feel at risk.
  • Upload-from-photos escape hatch for cracked cameras, bad light, and shaky hands. Never trap someone in a camera.

architecture

Images never touch Corridor's servers — the client uploads direct to the provider with a short-lived signed URL, and our backend only ever sees { sessionId, status, reasonCode }. That's a deliberate reduction of what a breach could expose, and it's the technical fact behind the promise made on 05. Capture is resumable: sessions survive backgrounding, so an interrupted verification doesn't restart at step one.

07

Recipient payout simulated validation real

Catch the failure here, where it costs a retype — not at payout, where it costs three days.

  • IBAN validated locally: TR length (26) + ISO 7064 mod-97 checksum, before any network call. Instant feedback, zero round trips, works offline.
  • The bank name is derived from the IBAN's own bank code (0062 → Garanti BBVA) and echoed back. That's the cheapest possible confirmation that the user typed the right account — they recognise the bank, or they don't.
  • The name-match warning is stated up front, with the actual consequence and the actual delay. It's the number one cause of Turkish payout rejection, and a user who reads this sentence checks the spelling.
  • "Exactly as their bank has it" beats "Full name" — it tells the user what correctness means here, which is not what it means anywhere else in the app.
  • The saved recipient is pre-selected and marked verified — a recipient who has successfully received once is the safest path, so it's the default.
  • Turkish placeholder text (Ad Soyad) — this corridor's users are Turkish speakers.

architecture

Local validation catches format errors; it cannot catch a valid IBAN belonging to the wrong person. Real implementations resolve that with a name-check service before debit where the market offers one. Turkey does not, so the design absorbs it: warn loudly at entry, mark previously-successful recipients as verified, and make the returned-payout path on 11 non-catastrophic. When you can't prevent a failure, make it cheap.

08

Review & confirm hybrid

The last screen where anything can be changed. Every number that will be charged is visible without tapping anything.

  • Nothing is behind an expander. On 03 the fee breakdown collapses because the user is still exploring; here they're committing, so it's all open. Same data, different disclosure, because the stakes changed.
  • Slide to send, not tap to send. Deliberate friction proportional to irreversibility — this is the moment money leaves. It also makes accidental sends essentially impossible.
  • The lock is under a minute and the strip has turned amber. The rate-expiry warning arrives before the failure, not as the failure — 11a is what happens if they ignore it.
  • Both the source and destination account are restated here, at the last responsible moment, in mono. Wrong-account sends are unrecoverable; wrong-account reviews cost one tap back.
  • The four-node route sets the expectation that 09 will fulfil — the user has already seen the map before they watch the dot move along it.

architecture

Submission carries an idempotency key generated when the screen mounts, so a retry on a flaky connection can never double-send. The server re-validates the quoteId against its own clock — a client with a frozen countdown still gets rejected. On expiry the transfer isn't discarded, it's re-quoted: same recipient, same amount, new rate, explicit re-confirmation.

09

In flight chain leg real fiat legs

Five real steps mapped to five real rails — not a progress bar pretending to know something.

  • Every step is a rail, and every completed step has a timestamp. A generic spinner tells the user nothing and tells a reviewing engineer less. This screen is the architecture diagram, rendered for a non-technical user.
  • The hash appears the instant it exists — mid-flight, not on the receipt. It's the verifiable artifact; making someone wait for success to see proof gets the sequencing backwards.
  • Confirmation count is shown (2 / 12). On Base that's seconds, but it's the honest unit for this step, and it's what makes the on-chain leg feel materially different from the two fiat legs around it.
  • Pending steps carry their expectation — "usually under 30 min" — so silence never reads as failure.
  • The gold dot from the identity is doing real work here, pulsing on the live node. The motion signature is load-bearing, not decoration.
  • The reference number and a help route sit in persistent chrome. Something going wrong is precisely when a user shouldn't have to hunt.

architecture

State is push, with polling as the floor — Bridge webhooks and chain subscriptions drive the timeline, and a backoff poll covers dropped sockets and cold starts. Steps are monotonic: the UI never walks a step backwards on out-of-order webhooks, because a user watching a completed step un-complete has just learned not to trust the screen. Each step also has a stall threshold; crossing it swaps the copy from "usually under 30 min" to a named, actionable delay rather than leaving the row silently spinning.

10

Receipt hash real

The proof artifact. This is the screen that gets screenshotted and sent to the person on the other end.

  • The tx hash is copyable and links out to BaseScan. This is the case study's evidence: a real, independently verifiable Base Sepolia transfer. Anyone reviewing the portfolio can check it without taking my word for anything.
  • Rate used and total fees are restated after the fact. A quote you can only see before you commit isn't a quote you can audit — and disputes are always after.
  • "Time in transit: 1d 19h 50m" — reported honestly rather than quietly omitted. ACH was the slow leg; the chain leg took seconds. That contrast is the argument for stablecoin rails, and hiding it would throw away the point.
  • Delivered amount is the hero, in the recipient's currency. The sender's mental model ends at "did she get the lira", so the receipt answers that first and everything else underneath.
  • "Send again" over "Done". Remittance is a habit — most senders are on a monthly cycle to the same person.
  • Share is in the nav bar. Sending the receipt to the recipient is the most common next action after closing.

architecture

The receipt renders from an immutable settled record, not from live state — once terminal, it never re-fetches and never changes. Downloadable as PDF with the same reference number, because this is the document that goes to a bank or an accountant. "Delivered" means the payout was confirmed by Bridge as credited, not merely submitted; the two are days apart when a Turkish bank holiday intervenes, and conflating them is how a receipt becomes a lie.

Where the money doesn't move

Happy paths are easy to design and prove nothing. These three are the failures this corridor actually produces — a rate that dies mid-flow, a document a provider won't accept, and a Turkish bank that bounces the payout. Each one has a rule: say what happened, say where the money is, say what to do next — in that order.
